Why Your Baby Won't Sleep Through the Night (And What Actually Helps)

Published May 7, 2026 · Updated September 18, 2026 · 7 min read

By PurposeLab Studio · Content reviewed by PurposeLab Studio on September 18, 2026 against the sources below. Not clinician reviewed; educational content, not medical advice.

On this page

    Start with safety. For every sleep, place your baby on their back on a firm, flat, separate surface with only a fitted sheet. Keep pillows, blankets, toys, bumpers, weighted items, devices, and cords out of the sleep area. Call emergency services if your baby stops breathing, turns blue or grey, is limp or unusually difficult to wake, or has another sudden life-threatening change.     Why Babies Wake Up at Night

    1. Their sleep cycles are short

    Infant sleep is organised differently from adult sleep and changes rapidly in the first year. Babies often move into lighter sleep and may wake for feeding, comfort, temperature, illness, or no obvious reason.

    2. They need to eat

    Young babies commonly wake to feed. How often and when a baby can safely go longer between feeds depends on age, growth, health, and how they are fed. Follow your paediatrician's or qualified feeding professional's advice rather than a generic age table, especially for a premature baby or one with feeding or weight-gain concerns.

    3. They don't know day from night

    Newborn circadian rhythms are still developing, so sleep may be spread across day and night. Regular daylight, ordinary daytime activity, and a quieter, dimmer night environment can gradually provide time cues.

    4. Sleep regressions are real

    Sleep can change during growth, illness, teething, travel, and new developmental skills. Popular "regression" ages are rough descriptions, not a medical schedule:

    • Around 3–5 months: Sleep organisation often changes, and waking may temporarily increase.
    • Later infancy: Separation distress, mobility, teething, or illness may affect settling.
    • Any age: A sudden change accompanied by pain, breathing trouble, fever, poor feeding, or unusual sleepiness deserves medical advice.

    What Actually Helps

    1. Build a consistent bedtime routine

    Babies learn through repetition. A predictable 15–20 minute routine signals "sleep is coming." Keep it simple:

    1. Dim the lights
    2. Warm bath (optional, if your baby enjoys it)
    3. Change into sleep clothes
    4. Feed
    5. Lullaby or white noise
    6. Into a safe sleep space when your baby is ready

    Repeat a short routine when practical. Some babies respond quickly and others do not; consistency can provide a cue, but it does not guarantee sleep on a timetable.

    2. Use white noise or sleep sounds

    As we cover in our article on the science of white noise and baby sleep, some families use background sound for two possible reasons:

    • Its steady quality may feel familiar to some babies
    • It may mask changes in household sound that could disturb sleep

    If you use a sound machine, place it as far from the sleep space as practical, keep devices and cords out of reach, use the lowest useful volume, and limit duration. Do not use sound to cover crying that needs attention.

    Some babies settle better with a deeper, less hissy sound, so brown and pink noise are worth trying if plain white noise doesn't seem to make a difference.

    3. Differentiate day and night

    Help your baby build a circadian rhythm:

    • Daytime: Natural daylight and ordinary interaction can provide daytime cues; keep naps comfortable rather than deliberately noisy.
    • Nighttime: Dim lights, minimal talking, no play. Night feeds should be boring, feed, change if needed, back to bed.

    These cues may help the baby's developing body clock over time.

    4. Watch for sleep cues

    Some babies are harder to settle when they have been awake too long. Possible sleep cues include:

    • Yawning
    • Rubbing eyes
    • Pulling ears
    • Looking away / losing interest
    • Fussiness

    When you notice a cluster of cues, try the routine. Cues differ between babies, and pulling ears can also occur with discomfort or illness, so consider the whole picture.

    5. Age-appropriate wake windows

    "Wake windows" shared online are estimates, not clinical rules. Use your baby's cues and total pattern rather than forcing a schedule. Ask your paediatrician about persistent difficulty waking, feeding, or settling.

    6. Be patient with yourself

    Sleep deprivation can feel overwhelming. Some reminders that may help:

    • There is no "right" method. What works for one baby doesn't work for another.
    • Responsive holding, rocking, or feeding can be part of care, but always return the baby to a safe sleep surface before you sleep.
    • Sleep usually changes with development, but there is a wide range and no guaranteed age for sleeping through.
    • If another trusted adult can help, taking shifts may give each caregiver a safer opportunity to rest.

    If you feel you may fall asleep while holding or feeding your baby, move away from sofas and armchairs and put the baby in their separate safe sleep space. If crying feels overwhelming, place the baby safely in the crib, step away briefly, and ask someone you trust for help. Never shake a baby.

    When to See a Doctor

    Most night waking is normal, but talk to your pediatrician if:

    • Your baby seems to be in pain, is difficult to console, feeds poorly, or has fewer wet nappies than usual
    • They snore loudly, work hard to breathe, or have pauses in breathing during sleep
    • They are not gaining weight as expected or you are unsure whether night feeds can be reduced
    • A sudden sleep change comes with fever, vomiting, unusual sleepiness, or another sign of illness
    Remember: Night waking is common and is not a parenting failure. Ask for practical or clinical support when you need it.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    When do babies sleep through the night?

    There's a wide normal range. Many babies don't reliably sleep through the night until around 6 months or later, and continued night waking well beyond that is still common.

    Why does my baby keep waking up at night?

    Infant sleep changes rapidly, and babies may wake for feeding, comfort, illness, temperature, development, or no obvious reason. Frequent waking can be common, but breathing problems, pain, poor feeding or growth, fever, or unusual sleepiness need medical advice.

    How can I help my baby sleep longer stretches?

    A simple routine, age-appropriate feeding, daytime light, and a calm night environment may help, but no method guarantees longer sleep. Always use a firm, flat, separate sleep surface and ask your paediatrician about feeding or health concerns.

    Is it normal for a 6-month-old to still wake at night?

    Yes, many babies still wake at 6 months and beyond. Contact your paediatrician if waking comes with breathing problems, pain, poor feeding or growth, unusual sleepiness, or another health concern.
