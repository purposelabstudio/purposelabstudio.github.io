The Science Behind White Noise and Baby Sleep

Published May 7, 2026 · Updated July 13, 2026 · 5 min read
On this page

    White noise is a steady sound carrying every audible frequency at equal energy, which is why it resembles the constant whoosh a baby heard in the womb. Researchers think it settles infants by masking sudden household noises and dampening the startle reflex. A 2014 study in Pediatrics, the journal of the American Academy of Pediatrics, measured how loud these machines actually get, and this page describes what it found.

    Every parent discovers it sooner or later: the vacuum cleaner, the car ride, the running tap. Certain sounds have an almost magical ability to calm a fussy baby. But why? And is it safe to use sound machines or apps for your baby's sleep?

    Why Babies Love Background Noise

    The womb is not a quiet place. For nine months, your baby was surrounded by constant sound: your heartbeat (around 80-90 dB), blood flowing through vessels, digestive sounds, and muffled voices from outside. Research estimates the ambient noise level in the womb at about 70-90 decibels, roughly the volume of a vacuum cleaner.

    When a baby is born into a suddenly quiet room, the silence is unfamiliar and can feel unsettling. Background sounds recreate the comfort of the womb, helping babies:

    • Fall asleep faster: A 2014 study found that 80% of newborns fell asleep within 5 minutes when exposed to white noise, compared to only 25% without it.
    • Sleep longer: Continuous sound masks sudden noises (doors, dogs, siblings) that would otherwise wake a light-sleeping baby.
    • Self-soothe: The steady, predictable sound reduces the startle reflex (Moro reflex) that often wakes newborns.

    White Noise vs. Pink Noise vs. Brown Noise

    Not all "noise" is the same. Here's what the terms actually mean:

    TypeSound ProfileExamplesBest For
    White noiseEqual energy at all frequencies, sounds "sharp" or "hissy"TV static, fan, air conditionerMasking sudden noises
    Pink noiseMore energy in lower frequencies, sounds softer, deeperSteady rain, wind through trees, waterfallMore natural, soothing for longer sleep
    Brown noiseEven more bass-heavy, deep and rumblingThunder, heavy rain, ocean wavesDeep relaxation

    Research suggests that pink noise may be more effective for sustained sleep because it more closely mimics the sound profile of the womb. However, many babies respond well to any consistent background sound. The key is consistency: the sound should be steady, not changing or startling.

    If you're weighing one against another, the practical difference between brown, white and pink noise comes down to how much bass you want and how much hiss the sleeper will tolerate.

    Is White Noise Safe for Babies?

    Yes, when used sensibly. A 2014 study in Pediatrics, the journal of the American Academy of Pediatrics, tested 14 infant sleep machines at maximum volume and measured them at 30, 100 and 200 cm (Hugh et al., Pediatrics 2014;133(4):677-81). Every machine exceeded 50 A-weighted decibels at 30 cm, which is the recommended noise limit for infants in hospital nurseries, and three exceeded 85 dBA. The authors recommend running them further away, quieter, and for less time:

    • Volume: Keep it low. 50 A-weighted decibels, about the level of a quiet conversation, is the limit recommended for infants in hospital nurseries and a sensible ceiling at home. Every machine in the study exceeded it at close range.
    • Distance: Put it as far from the crib as you practically can. The study measured at 30, 100 and 200 cm (about 1, 3 and 6.5 feet); the further away, the quieter it reaches your baby. A phone on a nightstand across the room is better than a machine on the cot rail.
    • Duration: Use a sleep timer. The baby doesn't need sound all night. Thirty to sixty minutes to fall asleep is often enough.
    • Not a crutch: As your baby grows, gradually reduce reliance on white noise so they learn to sleep in normal conditions too.

    Lullabies: More Than Just Music

    Lullabies have existed in every culture for thousands of years, and for good reason. Research shows that lullabies:

    • Lower heart rate and stress hormones in infants
    • Strengthen parent-baby bonding through the familiarity of a caregiver's voice or culturally familiar melody
    • Support language development: babies exposed to singing show stronger neural responses to speech patterns

    In India, traditional lullabies like "Chanda Mama" and regional songs carry cultural familiarity that imported English lullabies simply can't match. That's why we included Indian lullabies in Hushly, because your baby deserves sounds from their own world.

    Practical Tips for Parents

    1. Start with white or pink noise for newborns (0-3 months) when the womb-mimicking effect is strongest.
    2. Transition to gentler sounds like rain or lullabies as your baby grows older.
    3. Use a sleep timer: set it to turn off after 30-60 minutes.
    4. Keep volume low: if you have to raise your voice to talk over it, it's too loud.
    5. Be consistent: the same sound at the same time builds a sleep association, helping your baby understand it's time to sleep.
    6. Don't stress about "the perfect sound": every baby is different. Try a few and see what works.
    The best sound for your baby is whichever one helps them sleep. Don't overthink it. Try, observe, adjust.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is white noise safe for babies?

    Yes, when used sensibly. A 2014 study in Pediatrics, the journal of the American Academy of Pediatrics, found all 14 machines it tested exceeded 50 A-weighted decibels at 30 cm, the noise limit recommended for infants in hospital nurseries. Keep the volume low, put the machine as far from the crib as you can, and use it for sleep rather than around the clock. Ask your paediatrician if you are unsure.

    Why does white noise help babies sleep?

    It mimics the constant whooshing sound babies heard in the womb and masks sudden household noises, helping them fall asleep faster and stay asleep.

    How loud should white noise be for a baby?

    Aim below 50 A-weighted decibels, about the level of a quiet conversation. That is the limit recommended for infants in hospital nurseries, and a 2014 study in Pediatrics found every machine it tested exceeded it at 30 cm. Three exceeded 85 dBA, which risks hearing damage over long exposure.

    Should white noise play all night?

    Most babies only need it to fall asleep, and 30 to 60 minutes is usually enough. If you do leave it on, a low, safe volume for the whole sleep period is fine. If you prefer, use a timer so it fades once your baby is settled, either approach works.
