The Science Behind White Noise and Baby Sleep

Published May 7, 2026 · Updated September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

By PurposeLab Studio · Content reviewed by PurposeLab Studio on September 18, 2026 against the sources below. Not clinician reviewed; educational content, not medical advice.

On this page

    Sound does not make an unsafe sleep space safe. For every sleep, place your baby on their back on a firm, flat, separate surface with only a fitted sheet. Keep pillows, blankets, toys, bumpers, weighted items, devices, and cords out of the sleep area. If you use a sound machine, keep it far from the baby, set it to the lowest useful volume, and limit how long it plays.

    White noise is a steady sound with energy across the audible frequency range. It can mask sudden household sounds, and some babies appear to settle with it, but research has not established that it improves sleep for every infant. The clearest evidence is about avoiding excessive volume and close placement.

    Some parents notice that a fan, running water, or another steady sound seems to help their baby settle. That response varies. A sound machine is optional, not a treatment for sleep problems and not a way to reduce the risk of sudden infant death.

    Why Babies Love Background Noise

    One explanation is that steady sound resembles the continuous background sound before birth. Another is simpler: a constant sound can partly mask doors, traffic, voices, or other changes that might disturb sleep. These are plausible mechanisms, not guarantees.

    • Possible masking: Steady sound may make changes in background noise less noticeable.
    • Individual response: Some babies settle with it; others do not or may prefer a quieter room.
    • Limited evidence: Small studies do not establish an ideal sound, duration, or benefit for all babies.

    White Noise vs. Pink Noise vs. Brown Noise

    Not all "noise" is the same. Here's what the terms actually mean:

    TypeSound ProfileExamplesCommon reason people choose it
    White noiseEqual energy per frequency, perceived as "sharp" or "hissy"TV static, fan, air conditionerBroad sound masking
    Pink noiseLess energy at higher frequencies, perceived as softerSteady rain, wind through trees, waterfallA gentler sound profile
    Brown noiseStronger low-frequency emphasis, deep and rumblingHeavy rain, low waterfall, ocean surfLess high-frequency hiss

    There is not enough high-quality infant research to name white, pink, or brown noise as best for sleep. If you use one, choose a steady sound at a low level and follow the same placement and duration precautions for every colour.

    If you're weighing one against another, the practical difference between brown, white and pink noise comes down to how much bass you want and how much hiss the sleeper will tolerate.

    Is White Noise Safe for Babies?

    Safety depends on volume, distance, and duration. A 2014 study in Pediatrics tested 14 infant sleep machines at maximum volume and measured them at 30, 100, and 200 cm (Hugh et al., Pediatrics 2014;133(4):677-81). All could exceed the 50 dBA hospital-nursery reference level at close range, and some produced potentially harmful output. The authors and later AAP guidance support placing devices farther away, lowering volume, and limiting duration:

    • Volume: Use the lowest level that is useful; do not run a device at or near maximum.
    • Distance: Put the source as far from the baby's sleep space as practical, never in or attached to the crib, and keep cords out of reach.
    • Duration: Use a timer or turn it off when it is no longer needed rather than creating unnecessary continuous exposure.
    • Check the setting: Phone sound-meter apps are only estimates. If you are unsure about output or your baby's hearing, ask a paediatrician or audiologist.

    Lullabies: More Than Just Music

    Quiet singing can be part of a predictable bedtime routine and a moment of caregiver connection. Evidence does not support promising a particular sleep or developmental outcome from a lullaby alone. Possible reasons families use them include:

    • Familiarity: A caregiver's quiet voice can become part of the routine.
    • Connection: Singing offers calm, responsive interaction.
    • Flexibility: It needs no device and can stop once the baby is settled.

    Practical Tips for Parents

    1. Start with safe sleep: Back to sleep on a firm, flat, separate surface with an empty sleep space.
    2. Keep devices out of the crib: Place the speaker and all cords far away and out of reach.
    3. Use the lowest useful volume and avoid maximum settings.
    4. Limit duration: Use a timer or switch it off when it is no longer needed.
    5. Observe your baby: Stop if the sound seems distressing, and discuss hearing or sleep concerns with a paediatrician.
    White noise is optional. Low volume, distance, limited duration, and an otherwise safe sleep environment matter more than the colour of sound.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is white noise safe for babies?

    Risk depends on volume, distance, and duration. Keep the source far from the baby, use the lowest useful volume, limit how long it plays, and keep the device and cords outside the crib. Ask a paediatrician or audiologist if you are unsure.

    Why might white noise help babies sleep?

    A steady sound may mask sudden household noise, and some babies appear to settle with it. Evidence is limited, and it does not improve sleep for every infant.

    How loud should white noise be for a baby?

    Use the lowest level that is useful and place the source as far from the sleep space as practical. A 2014 Pediatrics study showed that infant sleep machines can produce excessive output at close range or maximum volume.

    Should white noise play all night?

    Evidence does not establish an ideal duration. A timer or switching it off when no longer needed reduces unnecessary exposure. If you use it for longer, keep the source distant and the volume low, and discuss concerns with your paediatrician.
